Reviewer #1: The paper presents an important empirical exercise with the stated goal of contributing to public policy debates on public security in Chile. However, in its current form, it reads more like a policy report than an academic article. While the data analysis is interesting and clearly engages with ongoing debates on crime in Chile, I am not convinced that the paper, as it stands, meets the standards for publication in JQC.

The authors claim three main contributions:

First, novel data. The authors argue that the panel dataset they construct represents a key contribution. While assembling new data can indeed be valuable, the dataset used here appears limited for the analysis of property crime. In particular, it does not allow for the examination of heterogeneity across socioeconomic conditions, population density, or other relevant contextual factors. Prior work (e.g., Barthelon and Kruger, 2011, JPE) has already made use of municipal-level crime data in Chile. By contrast, the spatial aggregation adopted in this paper is quite coarse, which constrains the analysis. It is difficult to see how such highly aggregated units would be used in practice by law enforcement agencies. The paper would be significantly strengthened by using more disaggregated data and engaging more directly with specific criminal contexts.

Second, novel measurement. The authors propose a new categorization of property crime, distinguishing "surprise" crimes as a separate category with distinct analytical implications. While I have no objection to the development of new classifications, establishing their analytical validity requires stronger empirical grounding. In particular, the authors should demonstrate that this categorization yields meaningful differences in outcomes, ideally by applying it to other datasets or revisiting prior studies. As it stands, the categorization appears somewhat ad hoc and driven by convenience. Properly validating this measurement strategy could itself constitute a separate paper.

Third, empirical estimations as contributions. The third contribution is presented in an unusual way: rather than emphasizing substantive findings, the paper frames the estimations themselves as contributions. This approach is reflected throughout (e.g., in the abstract and introduction). The paper devotes substantial attention to technical details, but does not sufficiently contrast its approach with existing practices (such as the use of crime counts or rates) or clearly articulate what new insights are gained by focusing on crime composition.

Taken together, the main findings closely mirror prevailing views in Chile—namely, that crime has changed qualitatively with the increased use of weapons and a shift from thefts toward robberies. If this is indeed the central message, the paper would benefit from a thorough rewrite that places less emphasis on methodological novelty and more on clearly articulating and substantiating its substantive contributions.

In addition, the discussion of underlying mechanisms is underdeveloped. The authors rely on broad theoretical frameworks (e.g., Becker's model, routine activity theory), but do not engage with more concrete and contextually relevant drivers of crime. For instance, the widespread adoption of smartphones—valuable, portable goods even among low-income populations—likely increases the returns to certain types of property crime. In many Latin American contexts, a single smartphone can represent a substantial share of monthly income. Moreover, the diffusion of such devices may also be linked to increases in crimes such as kidnappings and fraud. Similar patterns have been documented in other countries and should be considered.

Minor comments:

The abstract is difficult to follow and relies on undefined terminology (e.g., "violent events," "coercive events").
The introduction does not clearly present the main results nor adequately connect the paper's contributions to the literature.
The opening paragraph jumps directly into the Chilean political context (elections) without first situating the research question within the broader literature.
The paper does not discuss the role of smartphone diffusion, despite its likely relevance for property crime dynamics.
The small number of spatial units (16) severely limits the ability to capture variation in urban density, inequality, and spatial dynamics. It is unclear whether these units are operationally meaningful for policy or policing.
If homicides and property crimes follow different dynamics, the justification for using homicides as a benchmark is unclear.
The rationale for distinguishing "snatching" as a separate category is not well developed. How much does this distinction affect the results?
It is unclear whether the demographic analysis is informative at such a high level of aggregation. Are the results robust to alternative population measures (e.g., counts vs. rates)?
"CEP" is not defined when first introduced (line 2).
The concept of the "dark figure of crime" should be defined earlier (e.g., at the beginning of Section 2).
